⚖️Centrism

The Pragmatic Middle Path Centre

Take the best ideas from left and right — free markets where they work, public services where they are needed — and reject the extremes of both.

In simple words

Imagine a shopkeeper who orders stock based on what customers actually buy, not on a fixed ideology about how a shop should run. If selling imported goods cheaply helps customers, they sell imported goods. If local suppliers produce better bread, they stock local bread. Centrism works a bit like this in politics: pick what works, drop what does not, and resist the pull of any single theory. It sounds simple, but in a polarised world it is harder than it looks.

Centrism is not the same as having no views. Centrists typically support a free market economy with meaningful safety nets, strong state institutions and a willingness to adjust policy based on evidence. They differ from social democrats in being less committed to extensive public ownership, and from classical liberals in accepting a larger welfare state. In foreign policy they tend to favour alliances and multilateral organisations rather than either isolationism or aggressive expansion.

India's 1991 economic reforms — opening markets while keeping welfare programmes — are often cited as centrist. Brazil's Plano Real (1994) ended runaway inflation without dismantling social programmes. The Dutch Wassenaar Agreement (1982) brought unions, employers and government together to balance wage restraint with full employment.

Core beliefs

  • ⚖️Practical solutions: Choosing ideas that work, whether they come from the left or right.
  • 🔄Gradual progress: Fixing problems step-by-step rather than risking chaotic disruptions.
  • 🤝Balanced approach: Supporting business growth while keeping essential public services working.

When it worked

Inspiring example

India's 1991 economic reforms

India · 1991

By removing the licence raj and opening trade, India unlocked rapid growth — while keeping public-sector banks, food subsidies and rural programmes — a pragmatic balance that lifted hundreds of millions out of poverty.

Inspiring example

Brazil's Plano Real

Brazil · 1994

A cross-party economic plan introduced a new currency and tight fiscal rules, cutting monthly inflation from over 40% to under 2% within months and stabilising Brazil's economy without a social crisis.

Inspiring example

The Dutch Wassenaar Agreement

Netherlands · 1982

Unions agreed to limit wage rises in exchange for employers agreeing to create more part-time jobs. The 'polder model' of negotiated compromise brought Dutch unemployment down from over 10% to among the lowest in Europe.

When it went wrong

Cautionary example

Israel's election deadlock

Israel · 2019–2022

No side could build a stable majority, so coalitions kept collapsing; the country held five general elections in under four years and went over two years without a new budget.

Cautionary example

Lebanon's power-sharing vacuum

Lebanon · 2014–2016

Lebanon's system of sharing power among religious communities to satisfy all factions left the presidency empty for 29 months, paralysing government and basic services while economic problems mounted.

What supporters say

  • Pragmatic policies that blend market efficiency with public services can achieve strong growth and social stability at the same time.
  • Centrist governments are more likely to build broad coalitions and maintain stable institutions than governments on the ideological fringes.
  • Evidence-based adjustment means bad policies can be reversed before they do lasting damage — ideology does not lock a centrist government into a failed course.

What critics say

  • Centrism can end up as an unprincipled 'split the difference' that satisfies nobody and fails to address deep structural problems.
  • When times are genuinely bad — a serious depression, a social crisis — a centrist position of cautious moderation may not be bold enough to solve the problem.
  • Critics from both left and right argue that centrism mainly serves elites: it keeps inequality from being too extreme while not threatening the wealth of those at the top.

Famous figures

  • P.V. Narasimha Rao

    India · 1921–2004

    As Prime Minister (1991–1996), oversaw the liberalisation of India's economy — ending the licence raj and opening trade — while maintaining democratic institutions and welfare programmes.

  • Lal Bahadur Shastri

    India · 1904–1966

    Prime Minister (1964–1966) who balanced national security with economic caution, promoted the Green Revolution and coined the slogan 'Jai Jawan, Jai Kisan', holding together a broad national consensus.

  • Dwight D. Eisenhower

    USA · 1890–1969

    US President (1953–1961) who kept much of the New Deal welfare state intact, built the interstate highway system and warned against the 'military-industrial complex' — governing from a pragmatic centre-right.

Common questions

Is centrism just 'sitting on the fence'?

That is the most common criticism, but it misrepresents what centrism claims to be. A centrist is not someone who avoids all positions — they have clear views on institutions, markets, welfare and international cooperation. The difference is that they hold these views flexibly and are willing to revise them based on evidence and changing circumstances. The criticism has more force when a centrist simply delays a difficult decision without resolving it.

What is the difference between a centrist and a moderate?

'Moderate' usually means someone who holds their political views with less intensity — a moderate socialist or a moderate conservative. 'Centrist' means someone whose overall position is close to the middle of the political spectrum. A moderate can be moderate about quite extreme-sounding positions; a centrist by definition sits in the middle. In everyday conversation the two words are often used interchangeably.

Is India's politics centrist?

India has historically produced centrist governing coalitions that combine market economics with public welfare programmes. But the label does not fit perfectly: there are strong ideological traditions on the left (communists, socialists) and the right (Hindu nationalism, free-market liberals) that have shaped Indian policy. Commentators often describe the dominant tradition as 'pragmatic' rather than purely centrist — always balancing competing pressures rather than following a consistent middle path.
