🏘️Communitarianism

Community & Duty First Centre

Strong families, active neighbourhoods and shared community duties matter just as much as individual rights and free markets.

In simple words

Imagine a village where water has run short. The people do not wait for a private company to sell bottled water at high prices, nor do they wait for an engineer from the state capital. Instead, every family sends one person to dig contour trenches on the hillside, plant trees and build small check-dams. Within a few seasons, the wells fill up again. That spirit of shared responsibility and mutual duty is the core of communitarianism.

Communitarians argue that modern politics focuses too much on isolated individuals. Liberals talk constantly about personal rights; capitalists talk about buyers and sellers; socialists talk about the state and the working class. Communitarians say all three miss something vital: human beings are shaped by families, neighbourhoods, shared traditions and local communities. Without strong social ties, individuals become lonely and societies fall apart.

In India, community-led watershed work in drought-prone villages like Ralegan Siddhi transformed barren soil into fertile land through collective labour. In post-war Western Europe, Christian Democrats built welfare systems grounded in family support and civic associations. In Singapore, the Ethnic Integration Policy set housing quotas so that different ethnic groups live together in every neighbourhood.

Communitarianism is neither purely right-wing nor purely left-wing. It supports social safety nets like the left, but honours tradition, family stability and civic obligation like the right. Its guiding belief is that a good society cannot be built out of selfish individuals alone.

Core beliefs

  • 🏘️Community first: Strengthening family bonds, local charities, and civic responsibility.
  • ⚖️Rights paired with duties: Citizens have an obligation to care for neighbours and society.
  • 🤝Ethical economy: Fair living wages and businesses that respect local communities.

When it worked

Inspiring example

Western Europe's Christian Democracy

Western Europe · 1945–1960s

Post-war leaders built welfare states that supported families, funded church-run charities and schools, and balanced market competition with strong community institutions, helping rebuild stable democratic societies after the devastation of war.

Inspiring example

Ralegan Siddhi village regeneration

India · 1975 onwards

A drought-prone village in Maharashtra ended extreme poverty and water scarcity through shared community labour, tree planting and watershed management, making the village self-sufficient in food, water and clean energy without relying on large outside donors.

Inspiring example

Singapore's Ethnic Integration Policy

Singapore · 1989 onwards

The government set ethnic quotas for every public housing block to ensure Chinese, Malay and Indian citizens live side by side, preventing ethnic ghettos and fostering daily cross-cultural interaction and social peace.

When it went wrong

Cautionary example

Pakistan's jirga 'honour' rulings

Pakistan · present day

Unofficial village councils put 'community honour' above the law and punish couples who marry by choice; around 1,000 'honour killings' are reported in Pakistan each year.

Cautionary example

US Prohibition era

USA · 1920–1933

A nationwide ban on alcohol pushed by moral and religious community groups failed completely, creating a massive black market, fuelling violent criminal gangs led by figures like Al Capone, and corrupting law enforcement before being repealed.

What supporters say

  • Healthy families, schools and local civic groups provide emotional care, trust and support that neither private businesses nor distant state bureaucracies can deliver.
  • Emphasising social duties alongside individual rights stops society from decaying into selfish consumerism and loneliness.
  • Local communities know their own problems best and can solve issues like water shortage, elderly care and neighbourhood safety through cooperation.

What critics say

  • Tight-knit communities can become suffocating, intolerant and oppressive toward women, minorities, and anyone who wants to live differently.
  • Putting community customs above individual liberty has often been used to defend forced marriages, caste discrimination and vigilante justice.
  • Modern large-scale economies require mobile labour and nationwide laws, which traditional close-knit community models can struggle to accommodate.

Famous figures

  • Nanaji Deshmukh

    India · 1916–2010

    Social activist who retired from active politics to establish the Deendayal Research Institute in Chitrakoot, spearheading a comprehensive village self-reliance model covering health, literacy and dispute resolution across hundreds of villages.

  • Deendayal Upadhyaya

    India · 1916–1968

    Philosopher who developed Integral Humanism, rejecting both Western individualist capitalism and Marxist collectivism in favour of an indigenous Indian model centered on family, community cohesion and social harmony.

  • Amitai Etzioni

    USA · 1929–2023

    Sociologist who founded the Communitarian Network, arguing that Western societies had developed an obsession with individual rights while ignoring reciprocal civic obligations to families, neighbours and the wider public.

Common questions

How is communitarianism different from traditional conservatism?

While both value social order, family and community bonds, traditional conservatism focuses heavily on hierarchy, established authority and preserving historical institutions intact. Communitarianism is much more focused on voluntary grassroots cooperation, neighbourhood solidarity and shared civic duties. Many communitarians also support progressive economic policies, such as strong universal healthcare, worker representation and environmental protection, which traditional conservatives often oppose.

Are village panchayats in India an example of communitarianism?

Yes, traditional and constitutional panchayats embody many communitarian principles by resolving local disputes and managing shared village resources collectively. However, Indian reformers have also noted the dark side of traditional village authority: caste panchayats and khap panchayats have frequently enforced discriminatory caste rules and punished individuals for inter-caste marriages. For communitarianism to work justly, community cooperation must respect constitutional fundamental rights.

Does communitarianism oppose individual human rights?

Not necessarily. Most modern communitarians do not want to abolish individual human rights or civil liberties. Instead, they argue that rights cannot survive in a vacuum without corresponding responsibilities. If every citizen only demands their rights from the government but no one contributes to their neighbourhood, cares for elderly relatives or volunteers for local institutions, the very social fabric that makes rights possible breaks down.
