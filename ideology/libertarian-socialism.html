In simple words

Picture a dairy where the farmers who pour their milk in are also the owners. They vote on prices, hire their own managers and share the profits. Nobody from outside tells them what to do. This is close to the heart of libertarian socialism: workplaces and communities that run themselves, from the bottom up, without a boss class or a government dictating every decision.

Libertarian socialists differ from communists in one key way: they distrust the state as much as they distrust private bosses. A government that owns all factories, they argue, simply replaces one set of rulers with another. Instead, they want firms to be owned and managed by the workers inside them — cooperatives, communes and mutual associations. They differ from mainstream socialists by rejecting central planning and from anarchists mainly in allowing organised, democratic federations of cooperatives rather than no organisation at all.

Amul in India, the Mondragon network in Spain, and the kibbutzim in Israel are all real, long-running examples of this model. None is a perfect fit for the theory, but each shows that worker-owned enterprise can compete in a modern economy.

Core beliefs

  • ✊Worker-run cooperatives: Employees make workplace decisions together and share profits.
  • 🕊️No top-down rulers: Power stays with local community meetings, not distant politicians.
  • 🤝Mutual aid: People help each other voluntarily rather than through government force.

When it worked

Inspiring example

Mondragon cooperatives

Spain · 1956 onwards

A network of worker-owned firms started by a Basque priest now employs tens of thousands across manufacturing, retail and finance. Workers own shares and elect their managers.

Inspiring example

Amul dairy cooperative

India · 1946 onwards

Farmer-owned and farmer-managed, Amul grew through Operation Flood into the backbone of India's dairy industry, helping make India the world's largest milk producer and lifting millions of rural families.

Inspiring example

Israel's kibbutzim

Israel · 1910 onwards

Collective farms and communities run by their members through direct democracy. At their peak they combined agriculture, industry and social welfare within a single cooperative structure.

When it went wrong

Cautionary example

Yugoslavia's worker-run economy

Yugoslavia · 1950s–1980s

Workers' councils ran the factories, but they kept raising wages and borrowing instead of investing; by 1989 foreign debt was huge and inflation passed 1,000% a year.

Cautionary example

Seattle's CHAZ / CHOP zone

USA · 2020

A self-declared police-free autonomous zone lasted about three weeks. Two teenagers were fatally shot inside it, and the zone was cleared after mounting safety concerns.

What supporters say

  • When workers own the firm, they have a real stake in its success and are less likely to be exploited or laid off arbitrarily.
  • Cooperatives keep profits local — money circulates in the community rather than flowing to distant shareholders.
  • Self-managed communities make decisions about their own lives without waiting for instructions from a state or a corporation.

What critics say

  • Worker-owned firms can struggle to raise large amounts of capital, because outside investors have no ownership stake to attract them.
  • Democratic decision-making in the workplace can be slow; in fast-moving markets, firms that decide by committee may lose to rivals who act quickly.
  • Successful cooperatives often end up hiring non-member workers on ordinary contracts, quietly recreating the employer–employee divide the model is meant to remove.

Famous figures

  • Dr. Verghese Kurien

    India · 1921–2012

    The 'Father of the White Revolution', he built Amul into a farmer-owned cooperative empire that transformed India's dairy sector and became a model for cooperative enterprise worldwide.

  • Ela Bhatt

    India · 1933–2022

    Founded SEWA (Self-Employed Women's Association), a cooperative union of informal-sector women workers in Gujarat that gave millions of poor women a collective voice and shared services.

  • José María Arizmendiarrieta

    Spain · 1915–1976

    A Basque Catholic priest who founded the Mondragon cooperatives in 1956, starting with five workers in a small workshop that grew into one of the world's most admired cooperative groups.

Common questions

How is libertarian socialism different from communism?

The key difference is the role of the state. Communists want the government (or the party) to own and plan the economy. Libertarian socialists distrust the state just as much as they distrust private bosses — they want workers and communities to own and manage things directly, without a government in between. In communist states, the state became very powerful; libertarian socialists want to reduce state power, not hand it more.

Is Amul an example of libertarian socialism?

Partly. Amul is owned by the farmers who supply it, not by outside shareholders or the government, which fits the cooperative ideal. But it operates in an open market, works with government agencies and has professional managers — it is more 'cooperative capitalism' than pure libertarian socialism. It is a useful real-world example of how worker and producer ownership can work at scale.

Is libertarian socialism the same as anarchism?

They overlap but are not the same. Anarchism rejects all forms of hierarchy and the state entirely. Libertarian socialism is willing to organise cooperatives into federations, accept some rules agreed democratically, and may work through existing legal systems. Think of anarchism as the far edge of a spectrum that libertarian socialism sits close to, but not at.
