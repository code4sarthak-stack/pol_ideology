🚩Communism (Marxism–Leninism)

Total State Ownership Left

One government owns everything — farms, factories, banks — and distributes the goods so that no one is richer or poorer than anyone else.

In simple words

Imagine a village where the land, the mill, the well and all the tools belong to the whole village together. Nobody owns anything alone. Everyone works, and the harvest is shared equally — the doctor, the farmer and the carpenter all get the same. There is no landlord to pay rent to and no boss to pocket the profits. That is the basic picture communism paints.

Communists believe that private ownership of factories and land is the root of inequality. They want the government — acting for all workers — to own the big businesses, plan what to produce, set prices and divide the goods. This is different from socialism, which may allow some private ownership, and very different from social democracy, which keeps private business but taxes it heavily. Under communism, markets are replaced by a state plan.

The Soviet Union (1917–1991) tried this on a large scale. Cuba has followed it since 1959. In India, the state of Kerala elected the world's first communist government in 1957, though it worked within India's democracy and constitution, making it a very different model from the Soviet one.

Core beliefs

  • 🚫No private billionaires: Big industries, banks, and land belong to the public state.
  • 🤝Basic needs for all: Healthcare, housing, and jobs are guaranteed to every citizen.
  • 🏛️State-planned economy: The government sets production, wages, and prices.

When it worked

Inspiring example

Soviet industrialisation

USSR · 1920s–1961

A largely farming country built heavy industries within a generation and, in 1961, sent the first human into space. Life expectancy and literacy rose sharply.

Inspiring example

Cuba's literacy campaign

Cuba · 1961

Around 100,000 young volunteers fanned out to rural areas and taught over 700,000 adults to read and write in under a year, cutting the illiteracy rate from about 24% to under 4%.

Inspiring example

Kerala's first communist government

India · 1957–1959

The elected government passed land-reform laws to reduce the power of large landlords and expanded school enrolment, laying the ground for Kerala's later high literacy and health figures.

When it went wrong

Cautionary example

Mao's Great Leap Forward

China · 1958–1962

Government took over all farms and set impossible targets, causing a horrific famine that starved tens of millions.

Cautionary example

Cultural Revolution

China · 1966–1976

Schools and universities were shut for years, intellectuals were sent to farms for re-education, and millions of people were persecuted. China lost a decade of learning and economic progress.

What supporters say

  • Wealth produced by workers should go to workers, not to a small group of owners who did not do the labour.
  • A planned economy can direct resources to where they are needed most — housing, healthcare, food — rather than to whatever makes the most profit.
  • Without the pressure to make money, art, science and education could be pursued for their own value.

What critics say

  • History shows that planned economies produce less than market economies and are prone to shortages, because no central planner can gather and use all the information that millions of buyers and sellers hold.
  • Concentrating all power in one party removes the checks and balances that protect citizens; most communist states became dictatorships.
  • Taking away the reward of profit reduces the incentive to work hard, invent, or take risks, which tends to slow economic growth.

Famous figures

  • Karl Marx

    Germany / UK · 1818–1883

    Philosopher and economist who, with Friedrich Engels, wrote the Communist Manifesto (1848) and Das Kapital, the founding texts of communist thought.

  • E.M.S. Namboodiripad

    India · 1909–1998

    Led the world's first democratically elected communist government in Kerala (1957) and championed land reform and mass education within India's constitutional framework.

  • Jyoti Basu

    India · 1914–2010

    Chief Minister of West Bengal for 23 years (1977–2000), the longest-serving elected communist leader in any democracy, overseeing rural land reform called Operation Barga.

Common questions

Is communism the same as socialism?

No, though they are related. Socialists want workers or the state to control key industries, but many allow a mix of public and private ownership and accept democracy. Communism goes further: the state owns almost everything, markets are replaced by a central plan, and in Marxist theory the state itself eventually 'withers away' leaving a classless society. In practice, communist states have been more centralised and less democratic than socialist ones.

Has India ever had communist governments?

Yes — within the democratic system. Kerala first elected a communist government in 1957. West Bengal was governed by a left-front coalition led by communists from 1977 to 2011. Tripura also had a long period of communist rule. These governments held elections, accepted defeat and handed over power, making them very different from Soviet or Chinese communism.

Why did the Soviet Union collapse?

Several reasons combined. The planned economy became inefficient: factories produced what planners ordered, not what people needed. Military spending was very high. Oil prices fell in the 1980s, cutting the main source of foreign income. Reforms meant to fix the system loosened state control and allowed long-suppressed demands for freedom and independence to surface. By 1991 the Soviet state dissolved into 15 separate countries.
