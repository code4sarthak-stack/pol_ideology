🗽Libertarianism

Maximum Personal Freedom Centre-Right

Cut government to the bone — no unnecessary laws, no high taxes, no telling adults how to live — and let free people find their own way.

In simple words

Imagine a high street where you can open any shop you like without a licence, sell any product an adult wants to buy, keep almost all of what you earn, and live as you see fit as long as you do not harm anyone else. The government's only jobs are to stop violence, enforce contracts and guard the borders. It collects just enough tax to do those things and not a penny more. Everything else — schools, hospitals, roads, pensions — is provided by the market, charities or individuals who choose to contribute. This is the libertarian vision in its purest form.

Libertarians are further from the state than classical liberals. Where a classical liberal accepts roads, courts and perhaps some basic education as legitimate government functions, libertarians push further and ask whether even those are really necessary, or whether private alternatives could do the job better. There is a spectrum: minarchists want a very small 'night-watchman' state, while anarcho-capitalists want no state at all. Most libertarians in practice sit closer to the minarchist end.

Estonia's dramatic post-1991 reforms — a flat tax, digital government and minimal regulation — are often cited as one of the most successful libertarian-influenced reform programmes. India's software export industry grew with very little government direction. And the light-touch regulation of the US internet in the 1990s gave start-ups the freedom to grow into globally dominant companies.

Core beliefs

  • 🗽Maximum liberty: Complete freedom in personal choices, lifestyle, and speech.
  • 💸Drastically low taxes: Slashing government bureaucracy, spending, and regulations.
  • 🤝Voluntary agreements: Society runs entirely on free-market transactions and mutual consent.

When it worked

Inspiring example

Estonia's post-independence reforms

Estonia · 1992 onwards

A flat income tax, fast deregulation and e-government innovations turned a former Soviet state into one of Europe's most dynamic economies and a global model for digital public services.

Inspiring example

India's software export industry

India · 1990s onwards

With few licences required and little government direction, private software companies grew from nothing into a major employer and foreign-exchange earner, showing what happens when a new sector is left relatively free.

Inspiring example

Light-touch internet regulation

USA · 1990s

A conscious US policy of not regulating the early internet gave start-ups the freedom to experiment, fail and grow without seeking official permission, producing companies that transformed global communication and commerce.

When it went wrong

Cautionary example

US private healthcare & opioid crisis

USA · 1990s–2020s

Lack of government rules allowed private pharma firms to push addictive painkillers for profit, while medical bills bankrupt regular families.

Cautionary example

Texas power-grid failure

USA · 2021

The state's deregulated electricity grid, which was kept separate from national grids to avoid federal oversight, froze during an unusual winter storm. Millions lost power for days and the state counted 246 deaths, with billions of dollars in damage.

What supporters say

  • People know their own lives and needs better than any government official; letting them make their own choices produces better outcomes on average.
  • A small state leaves more money in working people's pockets rather than taking it through taxation and spending it according to politicians' priorities.
  • Voluntary exchange between consenting adults — whether in commerce, lifestyle or ideas — is more respectful of human dignity than rules imposed from above.

What critics say

  • Markets, left entirely alone, tend to under-provide goods that benefit society widely but are hard to charge for individually — clean air, basic research, disease control.
  • Without strong regulation, powerful corporations and wealthy individuals can dominate and exploit those with less bargaining power — workers, consumers, small communities.
  • The Texas grid failure and the opioid crisis show that deregulation can create severe harms when essential services or addictive products are involved.

Famous figures

  • Sharad Joshi

    India · 1935–2015

    Founder of the Shetkari Sanghatana farmers' movement, who argued that Indian farmers were exploited by state controls on prices, trade and markets, and campaigned for a liberalised agricultural economy.

  • Milton Friedman

    USA · 1912–2006

    Nobel Prize-winning economist who championed free markets, opposed price controls and central planning, and popularised the idea that 'there is no such thing as a free lunch' — all government spending has a cost.

  • Ayn Rand

    USA · 1905–1982

    Novelist and philosopher who developed 'Objectivism', arguing that rational self-interest is a virtue and that productive individuals should not be compelled to sacrifice for others — a philosophy that influenced generations of libertarian thinkers.

Common questions

Is libertarianism left-wing or right-wing?

Libertarianism cuts across the usual divide. On economics it is clearly right-of-centre — it opposes taxation, regulation and public ownership. But on social issues — drug legalisation, opposition to censorship, anti-war positions, freedom of lifestyle — libertarians often agree with the left. This is why the standard left–right scale does not work well for libertarianism; a two-dimensional compass (economic vs social freedom) captures it better. Libertarians claim a quadrant of their own: economically right, socially liberal.

Are there libertarians in India?

There is no mainstream libertarian party in India, but libertarian ideas have influenced Indian debates. The Swatantra Party (1959–1974) came closest to a classical liberal / libertarian party in Indian history. Sharad Joshi's Shetkari Sanghatana brought libertarian arguments about agricultural markets to a mass rural audience. Think tanks like the Centre for Civil Society promote libertarian-leaning policy research. The 1991 reforms also drew on the argument that freeing markets would help the poor more than controlling them.

What is the difference between a libertarian and an anarchist?

Both want less (or no) government, but they disagree on what should replace it. Libertarians generally accept private property and free markets as the replacement — a stateless or near-stateless society still governed by contracts, prices and voluntary exchange. Anarchists, particularly anarcho-communists or anarcho-syndicalists, reject private ownership of the means of production and want collective, non-hierarchical communities to replace both the state and the corporation. The two groups often dislike each other intensely despite agreeing that the state should shrink.
